What this calculator is for

Cash Conversion Cycle answers one question: how many days pass between the moment you pay a supplier and the moment a customer's payment reaches your bank. It takes three balance-sheet lines and turns each into days - how long stock sits before it sells, how long debtors take to settle, how long your own creditors let you wait - then nets the three into a single figure you can plan around. The decision it settles is how much short-term funding the business actually needs. Two shops holding the same stock at the same margin can sit forty days apart on this measure purely because of the credit terms each one negotiated, and the one on the longer cycle is the one that needs an overdraft. Reach for it when you set credit periods for customers, when you agree supplier terms, or when a lender wants a cash-credit limit justified in days rather than in rupees.

How the maths works

Inventory Days = Inventory ÷ COGS (yearly) × 365. Receivable Days = Receivables ÷ Revenue (yearly) × 365. Payable Days = Payables ÷ Purchases (yearly) × 365. Cash Conversion Cycle = Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days. In the worked example, stock of ₹6,00,000 against yearly COGS of ₹36,50,000 gives 60 inventory days; receivables of ₹9,00,000 against yearly revenue of ₹73,00,000 gives 45 receivable days; payables of ₹3,00,000 against yearly purchases of ₹36,50,000 gives 30 payable days. The cycle is 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 days, so seventy-five days of purchases have to be funded before your customers settle.

When to reach for it

  • A customer wants 60-day terms instead of the usual 30 and you need to cost the extra thirty days of funding before you agree.
  • You are asking a bank for a cash-credit limit and have to express the limit in days of purchases rather than a round number.
  • Profit looks healthy every month, yet the bank balance dips each time a large purchase order goes out.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing stock by revenue instead of COGS for inventory days. Revenue carries your margin on top of cost, so the answer comes out shorter than the stock is really sitting.
  • Using the 31 March balance sheet for a seasonal business. Stock and debtors are close to their lowest on that date, so the cycle flatters you for the other eleven months - average the quarter around your peak instead.
  • Stretching payables past the agreed due date to shorten the cycle. The days leave the calculation and return as a supplier demanding cash on delivery or cutting your credit line.

Ye calculator kis kaam ka hai

Cash Conversion Cycle ek hi sawaal ka jawab deta hai: supplier ko paisa dene ke din se lekar customer ka payment bank mein aane ke din tak kitne din lagte hain. Ye teen balance-sheet lines ko dinon mein badal deta hai - stock kitne din pada rehta hai, debtors kitne din mein settle karte hain, aur apne creditors aapko kitne din rukne dete hain - aur teeno ko jodkar ek aisa number bana deta hai jiske hisaab se planning ho sake. Ye page jo faisla settle karta hai wo hai business ko kitni short-term funding chahiye. Do dukaan same stock aur same margin par bhi sirf credit terms ki wajah se chalees din alag ho sakti hain, aur lambi cycle wali dukaan ko hi overdraft chahiye hota hai. Isko tab dekho jab customer ke credit period tay kar rahe ho, supplier terms par baat kar rahe ho, ya bank ko cash-credit limit rupees ki jagah days mein justify karni ho.

Ganit kaise chalta hai

Inventory Days = Inventory ÷ COGS (yearly) × 365. Receivable Days = Receivables ÷ Revenue (yearly) × 365. Payable Days = Payables ÷ Purchases (yearly) × 365. Cash Conversion Cycle = Inventory Days + Receivable Days − Payable Days. Example mein ₹6,00,000 stock aur ₹36,50,000 yearly COGS se 60 inventory days aate hain; ₹9,00,000 receivables aur ₹73,00,000 yearly revenue se 45 receivable days; ₹3,00,000 payables aur ₹36,50,000 yearly purchases se 30 payable days. Cycle = 60 + 45 − 30 = 75 din, matlab customers ke settle karne se pehle pachhattar din ki purchases fund karni padti hain.

Kab use karein

  • Customer 30 ki jagah 60 din ki terms maang raha hai aur haan bolne se pehle un extra tees din ka funding cost nikalna hai.
  • Bank se cash-credit limit maang rahe ho aur limit ko round figure ki jagah purchases ke dinon mein dikhana hai.
  • Har mahine profit theek dikhta hai, par har bade purchase order ke saath bank balance dip kar jaata hai.

Aam galtiyan

  • Inventory days ke liye COGS ki jagah revenue se divide karna. Revenue mein margin bhi juda hota hai, isliye jawab chhota aa jaata hai aur stock asal mein jitne din pada hai wo dikhta nahi.
  • Seasonal business mein 31 March ki balance sheet use karna. Us tareekh par stock aur debtors sabse kam hote hain, toh cycle baaki gyarah mahine ke liye achhi dikhti hai - peak ke aas-paas ke quarter ka average lo.
  • Cycle chhota dikhane ke liye payables ko due date se aage kheench dena. Din calculation se gayab ho jaate hain aur supplier cash-on-delivery maangne lagta hai ya credit line kaat deta hai.
