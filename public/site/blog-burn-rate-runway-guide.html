Burn Rate and Runway: How Long Can Your Startup Actually Survive?

The single number that decides whether you're fundraising from strength or from panic.

Growth & CashSeptember 30, 2026

Most founders can tell you their revenue to the rupee. Far fewer can tell you, confidently and immediately, how many months of cash they have left.

That second number is burn rate and runway, and it decides something important: whether you raise your next round from a position of strength or from a position of desperation. Investors can tell the difference instantly.

What is burn rate?

Burn rate is the rate at which your business spends cash. It is normally expressed monthly.

There are two versions, and confusing them is a common and costly mistake.

Gross burn

Total cash you spend each month, ignoring what comes in. If your monthly costs are ₹12,00,000, your gross burn is ₹12,00,000.

Net burn

What you actually lose each month, once revenue is counted. If you spend ₹12,00,000 and bring in ₹5,00,000, your net burn is ₹7,00,000.

Runway is calculated on net burn. Gross burn is useful for understanding your cost base, but net burn is what determines how long you survive.

One important edge case: if revenue exceeds costs, your net burn is negative. You are cash-flow positive, and you have no runway problem at all — you have a growth-investment decision instead.

The runway formula

Runway (in months) = Cash in bank ÷ Net monthly burn

Example: ₹84,00,000 in the bank, net burn of ₹7,00,000 per month.

Runway = ₹84,00,000 ÷ ₹7,00,000 = 12 months.

Simple. The difficulty is not the arithmetic — it is being honest about the inputs.

Three traps in the inputs

  1. Counting committed money as cash. A signed but unpaid contract is not cash. Neither is an invoice your customer has not paid. Use your actual bank balance.
  2. Using a best-case month. If one month had an unusually large order, your burn looks artificially low. Use a three-month rolling average.
  3. Forgetting irregular costs. Annual software renewals, insurance, bonuses, audits and tax payments do not appear in a normal month. Divide them by twelve and add them to your monthly burn, or your runway will quietly be shorter than you think.

What your runway length is telling you

  • Under 3 months — Critical. You are in survival mode. Cut costs immediately and explore bridge funding, revenue acceleration, or both. This is not the time to be optimistic.
  • 3 to 6 months — Tight. Fundraising from here is uncomfortable, because investors know you have limited options and will price accordingly.
  • 6 to 12 months — Workable, and the most common state for a startup between rounds. Manageable, but you should already be in market.
  • 12 to 18 months — Healthy. Enough time to run a proper process without panic, and enough leverage to say no to bad terms.
  • Over 18 months — Strong. You can be selective, and you can invest in growth rather than merely survive.

When to start raising

A useful rule: start raising when you have at least 9 to 12 months of runway, and aim to close with no less than 6 months remaining.

The reason is arithmetic, not pessimism. A typical seed or Series A round takes 3 to 6 months from first meeting to money in the bank — and it often takes longer than you expect, because diligence always uncovers something. If you start with 6 months, you will be negotiating your final weeks from a position of weakness.

Start earlier than feels necessary. It is the single highest-leverage thing you can do for the terms you get.

How to extend runway without breaking the business

There are only two levers: spend less, or earn more. Both are harder than they look, so be surgical.

On spending: cut things that do not touch revenue or your core product. Deferred hiring, renegotiated software contracts, paused experiments that were never going to pay back, and reduced office space are the usual candidates. Avoid cutting the sales capacity or product velocity that generates your revenue — that is a spiral, not a fix.

On earning: bring revenue forward. Offer annual prepayment at a modest discount — it costs you margin but buys cash today, which is often the better trade. Tighten collections so invoices are paid in 30 days rather than 60. Raise prices if you have been underpricing; a 10% increase flows almost entirely to cash.

On the balance sheet: a small bridge round from existing investors, or a working-capital line, can buy time without setting a new valuation. These are usually faster and less dilutive than a full round — but they are also a signal that you should be honest with yourself about why you need one.

Why investors ask about runway in the first meeting

When an investor asks how much runway you have, they are not just doing arithmetic. They are working out your negotiating position, your urgency, and whether you are building deliberately or reacting.

A founder who says "fourteen months, and here is the plan for the next nine" is in a completely different conversation from one who says "about four months and we are talking to everyone."

Know your number cold. Update it monthly. And treat a healthy runway as a strategic asset, because it is.

The short version

Net burn is what you lose each month after revenue. Runway is your cash divided by that number. Use real cash, a rolling average, and irregular costs divided across the year.

Then start raising with 9 to 12 months on the clock, not 4. It is the cheapest advantage available to you.
