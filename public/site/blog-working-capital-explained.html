How Much Working Capital Your Shop Needs

Working capital is the money that never sits still — here is how much your shop needs, and how much to borrow for it.

Growth & CashNovember 27, 2026

Every shop needs two kinds of money. One buys the rack, the freezer, the signboard — things you own once and use for years. The other is the money that is always in motion: it buys stock, pays rent and salary, and comes back when customers pay. That second kind is working capital, and a shop can be fully furnished, fully stocked and still be short of it.

This guide shows what working capital actually is, how to work out the amount your own shop needs, and how big a credit line to arrange so that one slow month does not stop the business.

What working capital is

Working capital is what your business owns in the short run minus what it owes in the short run. Both lists are the things that move within a year:

  • What you own: stock on the shelf, money customers still owe you, and cash in the bank.
  • What you owe: unpaid supplier bills, the month's rent, salaries due, and any short-term loan instalment.

The difference is the money that belongs to the business and keeps the daily work running. If the second list is bigger than the first, working capital is negative, and the shop is being run on other people's money — which works only until someone asks for it back.

Size it with days, not guesses

Most owners answer "how much do I need?" with a number that feels safe. A better answer comes from one figure: the monthly cash cost of running the shop, and the number of days your money stays tied up.

Take the cash you pay out in a month — stock, rent, salary, electricity, transport. Now count the days from the moment you pay for stock to the moment a customer's money reaches you. If that stretch is one month, the shop needs one month of running cost permanently parked in it. Two months of waiting means two months of cost.

A worked example

A small shop pays about ₹6,000 a month in cash costs. Its money is tied up for roughly 30 days, so it needs about ₹6,000 held in the business at all times. The balance sheet shows the same number from the other side:

  1. Stock on the shelf: ₹4,000.
  2. Money customers owe: ₹2,000.
  3. Cash in the bank: ₹1,000.
  4. Total owned in the short run: ₹7,000.
  5. Supplier bill still to pay: ₹1,000.
  6. Working capital: 7,000 − 1,000 = ₹6,000.

Two different methods, one answer. That is the amount the owner must be able to lose the use of without missing a payment. It is not spare cash and it is not profit — it is the floor the business stands on.

How much credit to arrange

Arrange a limit a little above the need, not far above it. If the need is ₹6,000, a limit of about ₹9,000 covers a festival rush or a customer who pays late by a few weeks. Anything beyond that tempts the owner to fund expansion, or a personal expense, with money that has to be repaid in months.

The cost is easy to see. At 12% a year, ₹9,000 fully drawn costs about ₹1,080 in interest, or ₹90 a month. That is affordable while the limit is used for a few weeks a year. It stops being affordable when the limit stays fully drawn every month, because then the shop is not borrowing for a gap, it is borrowing to survive.

What a stretched cycle looks like

Suppose the same shop's money now stays tied up for 45 days instead of 30. The need rises to ₹9,000, the whole limit, and there is no cushion left. The first late-paying customer then forces a choice between paying the supplier late and paying salary late. The sales figure may look healthy through all of this, which is why the days — not the sales — are the number to watch.

A monthly check

Once a month, write down three figures: stock plus customer dues plus cash, then supplier bills plus rent plus salaries due, then the difference. Compare it with last month. A working capital number that grows while profit stays flat means money is quietly moving into stock or into unpaid bills, and the fix is to shorten the days, not to borrow more.

Har dukaan ko do tarah ke paise chahiye. Ek woh, jisase raik, phreezar aur saainabord khareeda jaata hai — yani ek baar ka kharch, jo saalon chalta hai. Doosara woh, jo hamesha chalta rehta hai: usse maal aata hai, kiraya aur tanakhvaah chukaaee jaati hai, aur graahak ke paise aane par woh laut aata hai. Isi doosare paise ka naam varking kaipital hai. Dukaan poore saje ho, maal se bhare ho, phir bhi is paise ki kame rah sakate hai.

Yeh guide bataate hai ki varking kaipital asal mein kya hai, aapki dukaan ko kitna chahiye, aur kitni udhaar seema rakhane chahiye taaki ek dheema mahina kaam na rok de.

Varking kaipital kya hai

Varking kaipital yani kam samay mein karobar ke paas jo hai, usamen se kam samay mein jo dena hai, ghataakar bacha paisa. Dono soochiyon mein wahi cheezein aate hain jo ek saal ke andar hilate hain:

  • Aapke paas: raik par rakha maal, graahakon se aana baaki paisa, aur bank mein jama nakad.
  • Aap par baaki: saplaayar ke bil, mahine ka kiraya, tanakhvaah, aur chhote karz ki kist.

Dono ka antar woh paisa hai jo karobar ka apna hai aur roz ka kaam chalaata hai. Agar doosare sooche pehli se bade ho jaye, to varking kaipital maainas mein chala jaata hai, yani dukaan doosaron ke paise par chal rahi hai — aur yeh tabhe tak chalta hai jab tak koi woh paisa vaapas na maange.

Andaaze se nahi, dinon se naapie

Zyadatar maalik "kitna chahiye" ka jawab ek aise aankde se dete hain jo surakshit lagta hai. Behtar jawab do cheezon se nikalta hai: dukaan chalaane ka mahine ka nakad kharch, aur ve din jitne samay tak aapka paisa phansa rehta hai.

Mahine mein jo nakad jaata hai use jodie — maal, kiraya, tanakhvaah, bijli, bhaada. Ab un dinon ko ginie jo maal ke paise dene se lekar graahak ka paisa aane tak lagte hain. Agar yeh daur ek mahine ka hai, to dukaan ko hamesha ek mahine ka kharch apne andar roke rakhana padta hai. Do mahine ka intazaar ho, to do mahine ka kharch.

Ek udaharan

Ek chhoti dukaan ka mahine ka nakad kharch kareeb ₹6,000 hai. Uska paisa kareeb 30 din phansa rehta hai, isliye use har samay kareeb ₹6,000 karobar mein rakhana padta hai. Doosare taraf se dekhie to wahi aankda aise banata hai:

  1. Raik par maal: ₹4,000.
  2. Graahakon se aana baaki: ₹2,000.
  3. Bank mein nakad: ₹1,000.
  4. Kam samay mein kul apna: ₹7,000.
  5. Saplaayar ka baaki bil: ₹1,000.
  6. Varking kaipital: 7,000 − 1,000 = ₹6,000.

Do tarike, ek hi jawab. Itana paisa maalik ko aise rokna padta hai ki koi bhugataan na chhoote. Yeh na phaalatoo nakad hai aur na munafa — yeh woh farsh hai jis par dukaan khade hai.

Kitni udhaar seema rakhein

Seema zaroorat se thode upar rakhie, bahut upar nahi. Agar zaroorat ₹6,000 hai, to kareeb ₹9,000 ki seema tyohaar ki bheed ya kuch hafte der se paisa dene wale graahak ke liye kaafe hai. Isase zyada rakhane par maalik vistaar ya ghar ke kharch ko aise paise se karne lagta hai jo mahinon mein lautaana hai.

Keemat saaf dikhate hai. Saal ke 12% par ₹9,000 poore istemal karne ka byaj kareeb ₹1,080 saal ka, yani mahine ka kareeb ₹90 banata hai. Jab seema saal mein kuch hafte istemal ho, tab yeh theek hai. Jab seema har mahine poore khinche rahe, tab yeh bhaari padta hai, kyunki tab dukaan kisi antar ke liye nahi, guzaare ke liye udhaar le rahi hoti hai.

Lamba hota chakr kaisa dikhta hai

Maan lijiye usi dukaan ka paisa 30 ki jagah 45 din phansane lage. Zaroorat badhakar ₹9,000 ho jaati hai, yani poore seema, aur koi gunjaish nahi bachate. Phir pehla der se paisa dene wala graahak maalik ko saplaayar ka bhugataan der se karne ya tanakhvaah der se dene ke beech chunane par majaboor kar deta hai. Is poore samay bikri ka aankda theek dikhta rah sakta hai, iseelie bikri nahi, din dekhie.

Mahine mein ek baar jaanch

Mahine mein ek baar teen aankde likhie: maal jama graahakon ka baaki jama nakad, phir saplaayar ke bil jama kiraya jama tanakhvaah, aur dono ka antar. Pichhale mahine se tulna keejie. Munafa wahi rahe aur varking kaipital badhta jaye, to paisa chupchaap maal ya bakaaya bilon mein ja raha hai — isaka ilaaj zyada udhaar nahi, din kam karna hai.
