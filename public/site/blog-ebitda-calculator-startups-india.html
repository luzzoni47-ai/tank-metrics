Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate EBITDA for my startup?

Add back interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation to net profit, or simply subtract operating expenses from revenue. If revenue is ₹6 crore and operating costs are ₹4.5 crore, EBITDA is ₹1.5 crore.

Is EBITDA the same as profit?

No. EBITDA is operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Net profit is what remains after all of those are deducted.

What EBITDA multiple do Indian startups get?

It varies by sector, but many Indian businesses trade between 4x and 10x EBITDA. High-growth and asset-light companies sit at the higher end.

In Shark Tank India Season 1, Revamp Moto took ₹1 crore for just 1.5% equity, which prices the company near ₹66.7 crore. If that business earns ₹2 crore of EBITDA in a year, the deal values it at roughly 33x EBITDA, a number most Indian manufacturers would never see. Skippi Ice Pops, by contrast, gave 15% for ₹1 crore, a ₹6.67 crore valuation. Two cheques, two very different profit multiples. What is ebitda calculator for startups india?

An EBITDA calculator for startups India turns a simple profit and loss statement into the one number most investors look at first. EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. In plain words, it is operating profit before the accounting and financing items, which makes it the cleanest way to compare two companies that have different loans, different depreciation schedules and different tax positions.

To get it, start with revenue and subtract the operating expenses, which means salaries, rent, marketing, raw material and everything else needed to run the business day to day. What is left is EBITDA. It is not the same as net profit, because it ignores interest on loans, income tax, and the yearly write down of assets such as machinery or software. For a young Indian startup with a term loan and heavy equipment, the gap between EBITDA and net profit can be large.

This calculator also applies an EBITDA multiple to show what the profit might be worth. Indian businesses commonly trade at 4x to 10x EBITDA depending on the sector and growth, so the tool shows both a 5x and a 10x valuation band in ₹ Crores. Seeing the band next to the raw profit makes it clear why two founders with the same EBITDA can walk away with very different valuations, simply because one sits in a faster growing sector.

EBITDA is not a perfect number, and it is worth saying so. It ignores the interest a company pays on its loans, which is real cash leaving the bank every month, and it ignores the fact that machinery wears out and must eventually be replaced. So a healthy EBITDA with heavy debt can still be a fragile business. Use EBITDA to compare operating performance, but always look at cash and debt alongside it.

Formula

The formulaEBITDA = Revenue - Operating Expenses

Both figures in ₹ crore or lakh. The result is operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.

Why Indian Calculation is Different (US vs India)

Indian investors lean on EBITDA more than their US counterparts for one simple reason, many Indian businesses carry real debt. A factory or a cloud kitchen with a term loan has interest to pay, and EBITDA lets an investor judge the operating business before that burden. A US template that leads with net profit often hides the very number Indian sharks want.

Indian startups also differ in what counts as an expense. Founder salaries are often kept deliberately low, and many costs are paid personally and never booked, which flatters EBITDA. A careful founder adds those hidden costs back before showing the number to an angel, because a smart investor will find them anyway.

Depreciation matters more in India than in a software-only business, because plant, machinery and even delivery vehicles are common. EBITDA removes that noise, but a chartered accountant will still want to see the depreciation schedule before signing off on any valuation, and lenders will look at it too.

One more Indian habit is worth flagging. Family-run businesses often mix personal and company money, paying for school fees or a car from the business account and calling it a business cost. Before you present EBITDA to an angel, separate those items honestly, because a professional investor will normalise them anyway and your credibility is worth more than a flattering number.

Common Mistakes

  • Counting interest, tax or depreciation as operating expenses. Those are exactly the items EBITDA is meant to remove.
  • Leaving personal expenses out of the books. A founder's car or home internet paid by the company flatters EBITDA and hides the real cost base.
  • Applying a software multiple to a low-margin manufacturing business. The EBITDA multiple follows the sector.
