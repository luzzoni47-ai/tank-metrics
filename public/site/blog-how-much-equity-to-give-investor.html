Frequently asked questions

How much equity should I give an investor?

It depends on your valuation: Equity % = (Investment ÷ Valuation) × 100. At a ₹10 crore valuation, ₹1 crore is 10%, and at ₹25 crore it is only 4%.

Is giving more equity ever a good idea?

Early on, 15% to 20% is normal because the investor takes real risk, but giving more than 30% for a small cheque is expensive for your long-term ownership.

Does giving equity cost me control?

A 10% stake does not by itself hand over control, but a board seat and veto rights can come with the deal, so read the terms and not just the percentage.

On Shark Tank India, TagZ Foods walked in asking ₹70 lakh for just 1% equity, an implied ₹70 crore valuation, and closed the same cheque at 2.75%, a ₹25.45 crore valuation. The money did not change, only the equity did, and that single negotiation is the whole question behind this page. How much equity should you give an investor? This calculator takes your valuation and the cheque and returns the exact percentage, so you can answer before you are sitting across the table.

What is how much equity should i give to investor?

How much equity to give an investor is decided by your valuation, not by the size of the cheque. If your company is worth ₹10 crore and an investor puts in ₹1 crore, the fair equity is about 10%. If your company is worth ₹25 crore, the same ₹1 crore costs you only 4%. Raise your valuation and the equity for the same money falls, which is why founders spend so much time on valuation before they spend any on the term sheet.

There is a practical floor as well. Investors rarely accept much below 1% for a serious cheque, because a tiny stake is hard to track and harder to sell later. So even a very high valuation has a minimum equity below which the deal stops making sense for the other side, and a founder who ignores that ends up without a deal rather than with a cheap one.

The cleanest way to hold your equity is to arrive with your own valuation already worked out from revenue and margin. A founder who can show the arithmetic keeps far more of the company than one who simply accepts the first percentage on the table, and the readout stays in ₹ Lakhs and ₹ Crores throughout.

Why Indian Calculation is Different (US vs India)

A percentage on its own is never the whole Indian deal. Attached to the equity usually come a board seat, a right of first refusal and a liquidation preference, and each of those changes what the founder has really handed over. A tool that returns only a number misses the layer that Indian term sheets actually argue about.

The going rate also shifts with stage. Seed and angel rounds in India routinely see 15% to 20% change hands for a modest cheque, while a revenue-generating company with healthy margins can give 3% or 4% for the same amount. A founder who checks the norm for their stage before the meeting rarely overpays for capital.

The tax treatment depends on the route as well. Money coming in as a fresh issue of shares is not taxed in the founder's hands, but a founder selling part of an existing holding is a different transaction with a different bill, so how the round is structured matters as much as the percentage.

Common Mistakes

  • Fixing the equity first and the valuation second, which almost always ends with the founder giving away more than the round required.
  • Ignoring the ESOP pool that is carved out of the founder's own stake, so the stake you expect to keep is higher than the stake you actually hold.
  • Copying an equity number from a foreign deal that was priced on a different method, a different currency and a different set of investor rights.
